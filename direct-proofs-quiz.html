Self Assessment: Direct, Contraposition, and Contradiction Proofs

Frequently you have to prove a statement of the form “If . . ., then . . .”. If you need to do this, you have a variety of options on the type of proof you can try. Among them are a direct proof, a proof by contraposition, and a proof by contradiction.
A direct proof of the implication *p* → *q* proceeds by assuming that *p* is true and showing that this implies that *q* must also be true. That is, you assume that the hypothesis is true and try to construct a valid argument that leads to the conclusion. When you try to prove a theorem of the form *p* → *q*, you usually try a direct proof as the first method of attack.
A proof by contraposition of *p* → *q* is a direct proof of ¬*q* → ¬*p* (the contrapositive of *p* → *q*). Recall that *p* → *q* and its contrapositive are equivalent statements. The proof proceeds by assuming that *q* is false and showing that this implies that *p* must also be false. It is usually worthwhile to see whether a proof by contraposition will work once you can determine that there is no clear or easy direct proof. A proof by contraposition is often called an indirect proof.
A proof by contradiction of *p* → *q* begins by assuming that *p* is true, but the conclusion *q* is false. If it can be shown that this leads to a contradiction, then whenever *p* is true, *q* must also be true.
A theorem stated as a biconditional, *p* ↔ *q*, can be proved by showing that *p* → *q* and *q* → *p*. This is called an equivalence proof. To prove that three or more statements *p*~1~, *p*~2~, . . . , *p*~*n*~ are equivalent, you can prove *p*~1~ ↔ *p*~2~, *p*~2~ ↔ *p*~3~, . . . , *p*~*n* − 1~ ↔ *p*~*n*~. You can also prove enough of the implications so that all remaining implications can be obtained by “chaining together” some of the proved implications.
