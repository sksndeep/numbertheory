Self Assessment: Mathematical Induction

Mathematical induction is a technique for proving that a propositional function *P*(*n*) is true for all positive integers *n*. In order to construct a proof by mathematical induction you need to prove:
  1. basis step: show that *P*(1) is true, and
  2. inductive step: show that if *k* is an arbitrary positive integer, then *P*(*k*) → *P*(*k* + 1) is true. (Note that the implication *P*(*k*) → *P*(*k* + 1) must hold for all positive integers.)
Once you prove (1) and (2), you can conclude that *P*(*n*) is true for all positive integers.
In a sense, a proof by mathematical induction is a proof by cases. Showing (1) proves that the first case, *P*(1), is true. Showing (2) proves that a true case will always be followed by a true case: if a case, *P*(*k*), is true, then the next case, *P*(*k* + 1), must also be true. These two together show that all cases are true.
A common error in proofs using mathematical induction is failing to prove that the basis case *P*(1) is true or incorrectly writing *P*(1). Establishing only the inductive step does not prove the desired result. Another common error is not correctly writing the statements *P*(*k*) and *P*(*k* + 1).
Sometimes you want to prove a theorem that states that *P*(*n*) is true for all integers *n* with *n* ≥ *b*, where *b* is an integer other than 1. In such a case, you change the basis case from *P*(1) to *P*(*b*); the inductive step remains the same, except that you must show that it holds for all integers greater than or equal to *b*.
